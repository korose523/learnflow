## Abstract

Two core decisions in adaptive learning—"what difficulty to present next" and "where the difficulty prior comes from"—each rest on an untested premise: the former assumes that expected gains across difficulty levels are mutually uncorrelated; the latter assumes that offline large-language-model (LLM) difficulty annotations can serve as a reliable prior for online decisions. We turn both premises into testable empirical propositions and report measured results on four public learning logs. First, we replace the equidistant-linear difficulty assumption `linspace(-2,2,10)` of a self-built simulator with Junyi Academy's 16,217,311 interactions (72,758 users, 1,326 exercises, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), reconstructing the action space as real exercises grouped by expert difficulty. Empirical success rates are strictly monotone in the expert label (easy 0.7355 / normal 0.6328 / hard 0.6160), while ordered transitions exhibit 12.3 percentage points of climbing friction (hard→easy 0.7534 > easy→easy 0.7335 > easy→hard 0.6302), providing direct behavioural evidence for an ordinal action space with asymmetric transitions. Second, in a real-arm experiment calibrated on 52,733 users (500 simulations × T = 40), a policy with a correct ability prior scores 0.7023 and an inverted prior 0.6714; a `flow_zone` policy requiring no ability prior scores 0.7119, differing from the ability-aware policy by only about 1σ (not significant). Third, on a single locally deployed model (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, GGUF IQ3_S), we measure the LLM difficulty prior's reliability: on DBE-KT22 (212 items) an anchored absolute-rating protocol yields Spearman ρ = 0.2195 (p = 0.0013) and a batch-ranking + Bradley–Terry protocol yields ρ = 0.1808 (p = 0.0083; **the value after merging both return forms**; the enum-array form alone gives ρ = 0.1553, see the Table 5 split note in §5.4), both of the same order as the behavioural-evidence agreement with expert labels (ρ = 0.2207), and the two are not significantly different (item-level paired bootstrap Δ = 0.0385, 95% CI [−0.0652, 0.2193], p = 0.34); on XES3G5M (120 Chinese primary-school maths items) ρ = −0.038 (n.s.). A four-family × three-scale open-weight matrix (11 evaluated cells) confirms that this conclusion is bounded to the assessed matrix and not generalizable to "all LLMs". Fourth, reconnecting difficulty estimation to sequencing, we find that cold-start ranking gains depend strongly on the criterion, sample size, and signal-direction checks: under an in-pool criterion the multi-signal fusion gain is positive only at the smallest sample size (k = 10, +0.0271) and turns negative thereafter (k = 500, −0.0695); after removing a sign-error configuration the sample-size-adaptive shrinkage λ*(k) = 1/(1+(k/27.3)^0.895) gives a +6.447 pp in-pool gain at k = 10 but decays below 0.35 pp by k ≥ 250; switching to a held-out real-performance criterion reverses the fusion entirely (Junyi −0.0267 to −0.1222, DBE −0.1752 to −0.2113, fusion win rate 0.00). Our central conclusion—and the most important methodological warning—is that consistency gain ≠ predictive-power gain.

**Keywords**: ordinal action space; Lipschitz bandits; flow channel; retrievability; sequential decision making; single-model LLM difficulty annotation; cold-start sequencing; negative results

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Two skipped premises

"Which difficulty to give the student next" is the core decision of an adaptive learning system. The common engineering implementation is greedy exact-match: match the item's labelled difficulty to the target difficulty and take the first satisfying item. This is efficient but embeds a strong modelling assumption—that the expected gains of different difficulty levels are mutually uncorrelated, so each level can be treated as an independent arm. This assumption can fail on two falsifiable structural properties. First, difficulty is ordinal: adjacent-level items are contiguous in content, required skill, and cognitive load, so adjacent expected gains should satisfy some smoothness rather than being independent. Second, the learner's memory state is dynamic: the same item has a markedly different effective difficulty for a student "just learned" versus "not touched for two weeks"; treating difficulty as a static item property discards this information.

Where the difficulty prior comes from is the other skipped premise. The traditional approach relies on expert scoring or post-hoc item-response-theory (IRT) calibration—costly and slow. LLMs offer a tempting alternative: use offline LLM difficulty annotation as the prior for online adaptive decisions. This is engineering-attractive but rests on an unsettled premise—the literature splits into sharply opposed camps (see §1.3 and §2.5). Both camps stop at the annotation layer, answering "how highly correlated are LLM annotations with human annotations"; few answer the downstream question that engineering truly cares about: how much loss does this bias cause in adaptive decision making, and under what conditions does the prior hurt rather than help the decision.

### 1.2 From design to execution: what we actually did and why

This paper was executed from a pre-registered design. During design, each of the two主线 faced a concrete executability gap; closing these gaps constitutes the empirical core.

The first gap is the source of the action space. The original design modelled difficulty actions as a continuous or equally spaced ordinal set, generating latent difficulty with `linspace(-2,2,10)` in a self-built simulator. This is pedagogically reasonable but makes ordinality an assumed rather than a measured property. To close the gap we replaced the simulator's arms with Junyi Academy's real exercises—grouped by expert difficulty label (easy/normal/hard) within a level-3 knowledge strand—and fitted p(item, ability-decile) with stratified empirical success rates from 52,733 users with ≥20 attempts, freeing the strategy comparison from any assumed functional form of p. This replacement yielded an unexpected dividend: difficulty transitions themselves gave a measurable directional signal, which we report as the 12.3 pp "climbing friction".

The second gap is the measurability of the difficulty prior. The original plan was to set the machine-consensus threshold and decision-layer loss as a function of ensemble size. We discovered a more basic problem unresolved: on this one model, only two annotation protocols yield usable correlation estimates, and this paper's protocol-level conclusion rests on those two alone—the other two each fail for a different reason (one truncates the thinking text and labels from it; one has zero parse failures but its ranking is uncorrelated with expert labels)—and the two usable values (ρ = 0.2195 and 0.1808) are not significantly different. This means that before discussing "how high is the correlation" one must first answer "does this number come from a protocol that can produce a number at all". We therefore moved the execution重心 of sub-design two forward: audit protocol parseability first, then report cross-protocol comparison. This is not a selective deviation from the pre-registration but the natural execution of its go/no-go checkpoint: when a precondition fails, downstream flow should not start. §7 lists this deviation and the remaining unexecuted items.

We also added an experiment not in the original design but exposed during execution—cold-start difficulty-estimation-driven sequencing. The reason: when difficulty estimation is reconnected to sequencing, an estimator's quality is no longer decided by its correlation with a label but by its ability to rank items correctly within a pool; these two proved to be different in our measurements. This added experiment produced both our most engineering-valuable rule (λ shrinkage) and our most important negative result (criterion-dependence of the fusion gain).

### 1.3 Related work and positioning

Treating "difficulty/curriculum selection as a bandit" is not new. Castleman, Macar, and Salleb-Aouissi (2024, RLC) built a hierarchical MAB tutor (concept MAB + problem MAB + BKT + forgetting-decay MCM), simulating 1,500 students on ASSISTments, where the difficulty-aware version significantly outperformed the difficulty-agnostic and random versions, and open-sourced it. Lipschitz bandits have three important new branches: adversarial adaptive discretization (Podimata & Slivkins, COLT 2021), non-stationary Lipschitz bandits (Nguyen, Gaucher & Vernade, NeurIPS 2025), and lexicographic Lipschitz bandits (Xue et al., JMLR 26, 2025). Memory-state modelling is standard in knowledge tracing but has not entered the decision layer: MemoryKT (Lin et al., 2025), MLEKT (Qian et al., 2025), SAFFKT (Song et al., 2024), and TPR-KT (Zhang et al., 2025) use forgetting for prediction. We therefore narrow sub-design one's moat to one sentence: use retrievability as an MDP state for decision making, not for prediction; the rest (ordinal actions, regret-bound adaptation) is adaptation of existing theory, and we claim no new proof.

On the LLM difficulty-annotation side, the optimistic camp is represented by Ballon, Algaba, Verbeken, and Ginis (2025, LLMcompare): LLM pairwise comparison + Bradley–Terry estimates difficulty ranking, Pearson r ≥ 0.80 (n = 1876) versus human annotation, with < 6% decay under 10% injected noise. The pessimistic camp is represented by Li, Chen, Xiao, Chen, Jiao, and Zhou (2026): across 20+ models × 4 domains, mean Spearman ρ < 0.5 versus human difficulty, model self-assessment AUROC ≈ 0.55 (near random), and larger models are less aligned. Notably, the optimists evaluate "difficulty rankings generated from synthetic data" while the pessimists' ground truth comes from field testing—they measure different objects. We take no side between the two camps; instead we move the question one step earlier: before debating correlation magnitude, ask whether the number itself is stable. §5.4 shows this is not rhetorical.

On "optimal difficulty", Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia, and Cohen (2019, Nature Communications) derive an optimal training error rate of about 15.87% (optimal correct rate about 85%) under an SGD learning rule, giving the first computable anchor for "flow channel"/"desirable difficulty" intuitions. Our companion paper M1 re-examined this anchor on ASSISTments 2009–2010 and found that, at window width 20, the sliding-window error-rate distribution has only 21 discrete support points; the single-value mode is an error rate of 0.25 (success rate 75%, 30,899 windows), while the 80%–85% success band occupies only 19.64%. M1 explicitly states that this distribution is a description of experienced difficulty and is not a test of any candidate optimal value, and that "the measured mode falls in the 80–85% band" is refuted by the distribution itself. Our §5.3 `flow_zone` policy therefore aims at about 0.75 success rate—aligned with the single-value mode—not at the 80–85% band.

### 1.4 Contributions

Our contributions are all supported by measured numbers, organised along four主线 (plus an engineering-implementation line).

Methodologically we clarify our contribution scope up front: the truly generalizable contribution is the protocol-audit method—the triple criterion for protocol usability, bootstrap retest-uncertainty quantification, and the mandatory-reporting regime for LLM annotation experiments (protocol version, parse-failure rate, retest interval). The four-family × three-scale, 11-cell LLM difficulty-annotation evaluation is merely empirical support for this method; we make no general "LLM difficulty-prior boundary" claim (model-related conclusions are bounded to the assessed annotation matrix; see §5.8 and limitation ⑬).

**R1—Empirical basis of the ordinal action space.** Replacing the simulator's equidistant-linear difficulty with Junyi real exercises gives the three expert-difficulty empirical success rates (0.7355 / 0.6328 / 0.6160) and evidence of asymmetry in ordered transitions: hard→easy 0.7534 exceeds easy→easy 0.7335, while easy→hard is only 0.6302—a 12.3 pp gap between descending and ascending transitions. This "climbing friction" is first-hand behavioural evidence for the modelling premise "action space is ordinal with asymmetric transitions". We also report an inverse boundary: on XES3G5M the knowledge-component-tree depth vs. empirical difficulty has only ρ = +0.0785 and is non-monotone across strata; of 347 parent–child edges, 48.7% have parent easier than child—about a coin flip. The knowledge tree cannot serve as a proxy for difficulty order; the ordinal action space must be constructed from difficulty itself.

**R2—A sequencing rule needing no ability prior can match ability-aware policies.** In 500 simulations × T = 40, the difference between correct and inverted ability prior is +0.0309 (~2.9σ); the `flow_zone` policy needing no ability prior achieves 0.7119, higher than llm_correct's 0.7023 but not significantly (~1σ). We cautiously claim that M1's located single-value mode (75% success, error rate 0.25) can be operationalised as a sequencing rule that does not depend on ability estimation and matches ability-aware policies on real arms. We explicitly refuse to read this as "no need to model ability" and refuse to call the 80–85% band the "optimal success-rate zone".

**R3—Reliability measurement of local open-weight LLM difficulty priors, with two methodological corrections.** Under parseable protocols, on DBE-KT22 this model (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, GGUF IQ3_S) agrees with expert difficulty at ρ = 0.2195 (anchored absolute rating) / 0.1808 (enum-array + BT; **the value after merging both return forms**; the enum-array form alone gives ρ = 0.1553, see the Table 5 split note in §5.4), both of the same order as the 161,953-response behavioural evidence's agreement with expert labels (ρ = 0.2207), and the two are not significantly different (item-level paired bootstrap, BT refit within each resample: Δ = 0.0385, 95% CI [−0.0652, 0.2193], p = 0.34); on XES3G5M's Chinese maths, ρ = −0.038 (n.s.). This model's difficulty perception is item-type dependent and cannot unconditionally serve as a difficulty prior, and this conclusion is bounded to the assessed 4-family × 3-scale matrix (see §5.8 and limitation ④b); E1-B dual-protocol coverage and the S4 scale remain missing. Two methodological corrections must accompany the conclusion: (i) only two protocols yield usable estimates (enum-array + BT, anchored absolute rating); the other two are not citable and are not used as a contrast in the conclusion; the free-string protocol's failure is that its ranking is itself unreliable (zero parse failures but ρ = 0.0478 n.s.), not parse failure; (ii) an earlier claim that "0.046 came from 60 of 108 batches failing to parse" is withdrawn—batch-by-batch recomputation shows 0.046 came from the free-string form's 48 batches with zero parse failures, while parse failures (13 batches) all fell in the enum-array form; the real cause of 0.046 → 0.1808 is that the two return forms' records were merged. The engineering conclusion is narrowed to: `format: json` is insufficient to constrain structured output (the model emits `{"explanation": ...}` templates, 30% parse-failure rate); one must constrain the value set at the JSON-Schema enum/array-enum level, and ranking tasks must use enum arrays; protocol version and parse-failure rate must be mandatory report items.

**R4—Criterion-dependence of cold-start sequencing and a directly deployable shrinkage rule (co-reported with M1).** Under the expert-label criterion, multi-signal fusion on Junyi raises agreement from 0.2610 to 0.3629; but under a held-out-real-performance criterion the same fusion is worse than success rate alone on all sample sizes (Junyi −0.0267 to −0.1222, DBE −0.1752 to −0.2113, fusion win rate 0.00). Consistency gain ≠ predictive-power gain—this reversal is our most important negative result. We further located a real sign-error configuration in the fusion (upgrade-rate correlated −0.1837 with expert labels, −0.756 in-pool, yet assigned +0.154 positive weight); after removal, the sample-size-adaptive shrinkage λ*(k) = 1/(1+(k/27.3)^0.895) gives a +6.447 pp in-pool gain at k = 10 (this paper's口径, Table 7), decaying below 0.35 pp by k ≥ 250, and not reproduced on DBE. The held-out validation (record-level O7/O8, leave-one-k cross-validation of λ (in-interval interpolation) O9, student-level holdout O10, and srw7 O11/O12) is fully reported by companion M1 §7.2–7.8 (Tables 7-1, 7-2). We keep only the in-pool results (Tables 6, 7) belonging to the sequencing-decision side and the derived design judgements, and do not duplicate those held-out tables; readers should cite M1 for specific numbers.

**R5—Engineering落地 of conclusions.** Two conclusions are now backend code in the host system (LearnFlow): a new difficulty-metric layer `difficulty_fusion.py` (ECDF-logit rank fusion, invariant to any monotonic reparametrization) and a change replacing the >180s hard threshold in `optimal_difficulty.py` with a log-time continuous soft term `s = σ(ln(t/60)/0.8)` plus a hint penalty; both ship with 14 regression tests, raising the full test count from 920 to 936 (measured `pytest` 936 passed). The λ shrinkage and optimised estimator are landed as incremental functions (`estimate_optimized_difficulty()`), adding no new pytest cases, so the landing does not change the test count; its correctness is verified by the external script `o11_land_verify.py` (15/15 PASS).

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Bandits and RL item selection in education

Castleman et al. (2024)'s hierarchical MAB tutor is the most representative implementation: an upper MAB selects concepts, a lower MAB selects item difficulty, paired with BKT mastery estimation and MCM forgetting decay, achieving optimal mastery (~0.92) on an ASSISTments simulation. Its open-sourcing makes it an unavoidable baseline. Our difference from this work lies in two structural properties: ordinality (adjacent difficulty levels can borrow information) and memory state (retrievability entering the state). Note that our real-arm experiment did not reproduce Castleman et al.'s full hierarchical architecture (BKT/MCM modules); we limit the comparison to "strategy choice given real arms". The hierarchical MAB itself is implemented and reported as an external baseline in §5.3 (see §5.6 item ③ / §8 item ⑤): it is statistically indistinguishable from the best internal policy, and its faithful difficulty-weighting variant falls below random. On the knowledge-tracing side, Gan et al. (2020)'s KTM-DLF jointly models cognitive item difficulty with learning/forgetting curves to predict performance; our difficulty decision fixes the reward at immediate success rate and does not model learning/forgetting dynamics, so that line is a complementary background reference rather than a head-to-head opponent.

### 2.2 Three new branches of Lipschitz bandits

Lipschitz bandits assume Lipschitz continuity of the expected-reward function over the action space. Three recent branches are directly relevant: adversarial adaptive discretization (Podimata & Slivkins, COLT 2021) gives the first zooming algorithm and instance-dependent regret bound under adversarial rewards; non-stationary settings (Nguyen et al., NeurIPS 2025) give dynamic regret Õ(L̃^{1/3}T^{2/3}); lexicographic multi-objective (Xue et al., 2025, JMLR 26) gives a hierarchy-weighted regret bound and lower bound. The first two serve our main setting; the third serves compliance-priority concerns, which we do not expand. We produce no new regret bound; our contribution is to test whether difficulty selection satisfies these theories' applicability preconditions and to measure on real data whether the "ordinality" precondition holds.

### 2.3 Memory-state modelling: from prediction to decision

MemoryKT, MLEKT, SAFFKT, and TPR-KT already use forgetting and memory state to improve knowledge-tracing prediction accuracy, but have not entered the action-selection layer. This leaves a structural gap: since "the same student's effective difficulty on the same item varies with retrievability", optimal difficulty selection must depend on retrievability—yet prediction models only output "probability of next-item correctness", not "what difficulty to select now". §3.4 gives the formal接入 of this, but it is not tested this round (see §8).

### 2.4 Flow and optimal challenge

Flow theory posits optimal experience at the "challenge–skill balance", often engineered as a fixed correct-rate interval (e.g., [0.75, 0.85]). Rosas, Padilla-Zea, and Burgos (2026, Sensors) validate the balance axiom with physiological signals and nonlinear analysis while exposing internal contradictions in flow theory; Dell'Anna, Brunetto, and Gera (2026, LUMAT-B 11(2), Article 23) note that prior-difficulty-based recommendation may fail to sustain optimal experience and requires dynamic flow adjustment. Wilson et al. (2019) derive the computable anchor of 85% from learning rules. We therefore recast the flow zone from a fixed interval to a two-dimensional region characterised by its non-dominated boundary, testing its non-degeneracy in the simulator (§5.1) and its operationalised policy performance on real arms (§5.3).

### 2.5 Two camps on LLM difficulty annotation and a middle ground

The optimistic camp (Ballon et al., 2025) uses LLM pairwise comparison + Bradley–Terry aggregation to estimate difficulty without ground truth; r ≥ 0.80 and robustness to noise are the main evidence, but note their evaluation scenario is "difficulty rankings generated from synthetic data". The pessimistic camp (Li et al., 2026) finds three core results: cross-domain mean Spearman ρ < 0.5; model self-assessment vs. true performance AUROC ≈ 0.55; and larger models are less aligned, converging on "machine consensus"—the third directly negates the common engineering practice of "using multi-model voting to raise confidence". The middle camp (Liu, Bhandari & Pardos, 2025, BJET) treats 6 LLMs as synthetic subjects, reporting GPT-3.5 vs. human difficulty ranking ρ = 0.87, raisable from 0.89 to 0.93 with mixed resampling at only 50 human samples, but also finding a "narrow-distribution trap"—synthetic subjects' ability distribution is too narrow, systematically underestimating discriminability.

Easy2Hard-Bench (Ding et al., 2024) uses collective LLM performance as a difficulty proxy and finds IRT results fit human preference better than GPT-4's own ranking, creating a key tension: optimists say "ensemble is better", pessimists say "collective converges on machine consensus". Pratiwi and Fakhrurroja (2025) use Gemini 2.5 Pro to label 505 items' Bloom levels, reporting 88% agreement with manual validation and 94% inter-model agreement, but self-acknowledge a single validator and no inter-rater reliability. Parfenova et al. (2025, Findings of NAACL 2025) find LLMs closer to coding gold standards yet rated lower by humans. Together these suggest: high inter-model agreement ≠ high model–human agreement. Our §5.4 contribution is not to join one camp but to point out: before debating correlation magnitude, one must ensure the correlation measurement itself is protocol-robust.

### 2.6 Precedents and gaps for ordinal action spaces in education and recommendation

Treating the action space as ordinal rather than unordered has precedents outside education: adversarial Lipschitz bandits are used for dynamic pricing and auction reserve-price setting (Podimata & Slivkins, COLT 2021), where the action (price) is naturally ordinal and adjacent prices' expected returns satisfy smoothness—showing "ordinal action + smooth reward" is a widely accepted modelling choice. Education's specificity is twofold: the reward is not immediate gain but delayed, indirectly observable learning gain, characterised by dose–response and memory models; and action effect depends on the learner's memory state, which evolves across sessions. The difficulty is not the action space but the availability of reward and state. §5.2 proves the measurability of the first point with real transition data; §8 admits the second point is not tested this round.

## 3 Problem Formalization

### 3.1 Elements of sequential decision making

Define difficulty selection as an MDP: state s_t = (θ_t, {R_k(t)}_k, e_t), where θ_t is ability estimate, R_k(t) is knowledge-point k's retrievability, e_t is recent effort/time-cost features; action d_t ∈ D ⊆ ℝ (difficulty level), D an ordinal discrete or continuous set; reward r_t = learning gain (e.g., mastery increment or next-window post-hoc correctness gain), not whether this step is correct; transition: ability updates by learning gain, retrievability monotonically decays over time by a memory model and rises with successful review.

We must confess a口径 gap: in §5.1's simulator experiment the reward is the designer-defined learning gain; in §5.2–5.3's real-arm experiment the only available reward is immediate success rate, which is not a sufficient proxy for learning gain. We therefore refuse all teaching-effect interpretations in §5.3, reporting only relative strategy performance, and list this gap as §8's primary limitation.

### 3.2 Ordinal action space and regret-bound adaptation

**Precondition L (Lipschitz continuity):** ∃ L > 0 such that |μ(d) − μ(d′)| ≤ L|d − d′| for all d, d′ ∈ D, where μ(·) is the expected learning-gain function. Under L, the continuous-arm bandit (Zooming and HOO) regret bound is Õ(T^{(d̄+1)/(d̄+2)}), with d̄ the effective dimension; the one-dimensional case is about Õ(T^{2/3}), better than independent-arm UCB's Õ(√(K·T)) for large K.

**Separation of L's estimation and L's testing (avoiding circularity).** Using the same quantity to both estimate L and test precondition L would be circular; our test deliberately does not depend on the same quantity: L is estimated by cross-sectional regression of adjacent-level gain differences, while L's test uses two independent pieces of evidence—subadditivity of gain differences with level distance, and cross-session extrapolation error. **Current status:** this test is not executed on real data (§8). On real data we do a weaker but more basic thing—test whether the more primitive property "ordinality" is observable, i.e., whether difficulty transitions show directional structure (§5.2). If ordinality itself is unobservable, estimating L on top of it is meaningless.

**Non-stationary and adversarial extensions.** Learner ability drifts within a session, making the reward distribution non-stationary. Our main setting adopts Nguyen et al. (NeurIPS 2025)'s non-stationary Lipschitz bandit, giving dynamic regret Õ(L̃^{1/3}T^{2/3}); the robust setting adopts Podimata & Slivkins (COLT 2021)'s adversarial zooming. **The expected benefit of this part is not validated on real data.**

### 3.3 Two-dimensional calibrable formalization of the flow channel

Define the feasible set Φ = { (d, e) : ΔGain(d) ≥ g₀ and Cost(e) ≤ c₀ }, where ΔGain(d) is the expected gain in the prior-error-rate direction and Cost(e) is effort/time cost (characterised by reaction-time z-score, hint-use count, and dropout-rate synthesis). Φ is a region formed by the intersection of two inequalities, not a curve. The theoretically meaningful part is its non-dominated set ∂Φ⁺ = { (d,e) ∈ Φ : ¬∃(d′,e′) ∈ Φ with ΔGain(d′) ≥ ΔGain(d) and Cost(e′) ≤ Cost(e), at least one strict }.

**Falsifiable proposition F:** ∂Φ⁺ is non-degenerate, i.e., shows a negative slope in the (error-rate, cost) plane—a real gain–cost trade-off exists; the commonly used fixed interval [0.75, 0.85] is merely the projection of ∂Φ⁺ when the cost dimension is ignored. §5.1 tests proposition F in the simulator and obtains an important boundary condition on the cost口径: the boundary is non-degenerate when cost is "rounds to target", but degenerates to a single point when cost is "time per item".

### 3.4 Retrievability as an MDP state (not tested this round; non-stationarity precondition diagnosed 2026-09-24)

Treat R_k(t) as a state component. The effective difficulty of item j on knowledge point k is β_eff(j,t) = β_j − λ·R_k(t), λ ≥ 0 (schematic), i.e., high retrievability equals reduced effective difficulty. The action-value function Q(s,d) thus explicitly depends on {R_k(t)}. This yields two structural predictions.

**Proposition M1 (monotonic drift of optimal difficulty).** Let action d denote the intrinsic difficulty β_j of the selected item, with μ(·) unimodal. Under monotonic decay of R_k(t), the optimal action d*(s) monotonically decreases as R_k(t) decreases: more forgetting means higher effective difficulty for the same item, so to keep effective difficulty near μ(·)'s peak the system must switch to intrinsically easier items. This direction is directly refutable—if the opposite drift is observed, either λ ≤ 0 or μ(·) is not unimodal.

**Proposition M2 (review-vs-advance switching).** When R_k(t) exceeds a threshold, inserting a review item on this knowledge point has lower marginal value than presenting a new knowledge point; below the threshold the conclusion reverses.

**Difference from prediction lines (key sentence).** MemoryKT/MLEKT/SAFFKT use forgetting for predicting next-item correctness; we use retrievability for deciding—computing action value Q(s,d) and thereby selecting difficulty. **M1/M2 are not tested this round** (§8 item ③); the formalization is retained only to delimit our unfinished boundary; readers should not read it as a supported conclusion.

**Non-stationarity empirical diagnosis (2026-09-24 note, does not replace M1/M2 testing).** To test the stationarity precondition that this section relies on (treating retrievability/success rate as stationary MDP state and stationary reward), we ran two diagnostics on Junyi's real per-attempt log (Log_Problem.csv; 1,326 core items, 72,758 students, 16,217,311 per-attempt responses, with real calendar time and real within-student attempt sequence): (A) split early/late windows by calendar-time median and compare success-rate difference Δ_A; (B) compare first vs. second half by within-student attempt sequence, Δ_B; both with bootstrap (B=1000, seed=42) 95% CI. The results diverge: the calendar-time axis shows 741/1326 (55.9%) items with significant drift but only Δ_A = +0.0028 (~0.3 pp/year), practically negligible; the attempt-sequence axis shows 1326/1326 (100%) items with significant drift, Δ_B = −0.4362 (±0.0033), i.e., the same item's first-half success rate averages ~44 pp higher than its second half—the real strong attempt-sequence dependence of "first-encounter → retry-after-failure" mastery learning. **Implication:** M3's stationary-reward assumption is explicitly violated by the data on the attempt-sequence axis (the MDP's natural time step), and approximately holds on the calendar-time dimension; the formalization of propositions M1/M2 only characterises a structural assumption, and its "stationary" precondition must be read under this boundary, not as data-supported. Non-stationary handling's theoretical background can cite Nguyen et al. (2025); but this paper did not implement or compare their MDBE non-stationary Lipschitz algorithm (strictly observing review remediation K6). Diagnostic scripts and numbers are in `results/m3/r09_nonstationary/`.

### 4.2 From linspace to real arms: calibrating the action space

The original simulator's action was latent difficulty δ_d = linspace(−2,2,10) in ten levels, success probability p_succ(m,d) = σ(α(4m−2−δ_d)), α = 1.5. This is a reasonable setting, not a fit; its parameter magnitudes come from pedagogical common sense (single-round gain η = 0.06 lets mastery rise from ~0.1 to 1 within 120 rounds; forgetting rate λ = 0.015 is weak forgetting), and we keep this honesty statement when citing its numbers in §5.1.

The real-arm construction: define the action as real exercises grouped by expert difficulty within a level-3 knowledge strand (easy/normal/hard as three arms), and fit p(item, ability-decile) with stratified empirical success rates from 52,733 users with ≥20 attempts, reproducing the original simulator's strategy comparison without assuming any functional form of p. This replacement also yields a by-product: because every interaction carries the previous item's difficulty label, we can directly measure the empirical success rate of difficulty-transition pairs, turning "ordinality" from assumption into observable.

A negative boundary must be stated: we tried using XES3G5M's KC-tree depth as an ordinal-difficulty proxy, and the result was negative—depth vs. empirical difficulty Spearman ρ = +0.0785 (p = 5.1e−08, significant but extremely weak) and non-monotone across strata (depth-4 success 0.799 actually higher than depth-3's 0.746); upgrading "depth correlation" to a tree-edge direction-consistency test, of 347 parent–child edges 48.7% have parent easier than child—about a coin flip. Both pieces of evidence jointly refute "knowledge tree structure = difficulty order"; therefore our ordinal action space must be constructed from difficulty itself, not from a structural proxy.

### 4.3 Cold-start difficulty estimator and λ shrinkage

In the cold-start scenario the system must estimate an item's difficulty and rank by it with only k observations per item. Our single-signal estimator uniformly adopts O1's quantile metric: first empirical-CDF transform the signal then take logit (positive = hard), i.e., z_s = logit(ECDF_s(x)). This metric is invariant to any monotonic reparametrization, the key to avoiding "fusion effective-weight drift with scale"—on assist09, linear min-max fusion's effective-weight drift L1 mean is 0.5357, while quantile-logit fusion is 0.0001, with rank-reversal rate zero.

The multi-signal estimator adopts sign-correction + reliability-weighting seven-signal fusion. The early configuration (O8) dropped the "upgrade rate" column entirely (fused6), on the grounds of its negative correlation with difficulty; the §5.6 O11/O12 experiments show this handling is suboptimal: correcting the upgrade-rate's direction and counting it as negative evidence outperforms dropping the column; adding reliability weighting further improves. The final estimator is:

  score(item) = (1 − λ) · z_success(item) + λ · Σ_{s ∈ 7 signals} w_s · sgn_s · z_s(item)

  sgn_s = sign(Spearman(z_s, z_success))    # direction correction; success itself sgn = +1
  w_s   = max(0, split_half_reliability_s) normalized   # reliability estimated within fit block A
  λ = 1 / (1 + (k / 27.3)^0.895)            # full-fit 27.4 / 0.902, nearly identical
  signal set = all 7 (including upgrade rate, corrected to negative contribution)
  z_s = logit(ECDF_s(x))                     # O1's quantile metric

Here λ shrinks with the item's cumulative observation count k. Signs, weights, and λ are all estimated only within fit block A; criterion block B does not participate, so there is no label leakage; its held-out reproduction is in §5.6, and the per-k fit and in-interval interpolation test (leave-one-k cross-validation) are in §5.5. The L1 drift numbers cited here (linear min-max fusion 0.5357, quantile-logit fusion 0.0001) are unchanged.

Climbing friction provides first-hand behavioural evidence for "ordinal action space + asymmetric transitions", and is the substance of R1. But two qualifications must be given. **Comparison with the RL route (Fu et al., 2025, RL-DKT).** Recent work connects RL directly to dynamic knowledge tracing (RL-DKT), with knowledge state as state, item selection as action, mastery gain as reward. We therefore list "whether connecting RL directly to KT beats our cold-start alternative rule" as an open question in §8, not claiming to have refuted RL-DKT by not doing the experiment.

**R10 same-protocol head-to-head (run 2026-09-24).** To close the above "dangling comparison", we implemented RL-DKT's *policy* as a tabular Q-learning agent and ran it under the **exact same** protocol as this section's v2 / Castleman HMAB baseline: Junyi real-item arms, ability-decile conditional success probability p, T=40 steps, 500 sims, seed=42, clamp([0.05,0.98]), reward = **immediate success rate (not learning gain)**, PYTHONHASHSEED safe (strand ordering + per-sim crc32 seed). ### 5.4 Reliability measurement of a single-model difficulty annotation and protocol parseability

**All numbers in this section come from the same model** (`qwen36:latest`, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, GGUF IQ3_S; identity verification in §4.4 and `results/code/record_llm_model.py`). The title uses "single model" not "LLM" deliberately: this section measures **this model's reliability on these two item types**, not "the boundary of LLM".

**E1-A: DBE-KT22, anchored-scale absolute rating.** On 212 items with expert difficulty annotations, schema-constrained parse rate is 100%, Spearman ρ = **0.2195** (p = 0.0013), accuracy 0.4575, Cohen κ = 0.0651. As comparison, on the same items the **empirical difficulty** (from 161,953 responses) vs. expert labels has ρ = 0.2207. **This model's difficulty signal extracted from item text is of the same order as the 161,953-response behavioural evidence's agreement with expert labels.**

One honesty qualification: the model's marginal distribution is still heavily biased toward easy (138 / 65 / 9) while the expert distribution is 95 / 84 / 33, so κ is low (0.0651); the rank signal is real, but **absolute levels cannot be directly adopted**, only used as a ranking prior.

**E1-B: batch ranking + Bradley–Terry, stratified by return form.** This protocol has 108 batch records; stratified by **return form** and recomputed gives two distinct results (`results/code/o13_llm_protocol_audit.py`, product `o13_llm_protocol_audit.json`): **free-string** form 48 batches, **0 parse failures**, 676 pairwise constraints, ρ = **0.0478 (p = 0.489, n.s.)**; **enum-array** form 60 batches, 13 parse failures (21.67%), 697 constraints, ρ = **0.1553 (p = 0.024)**. After merging both forms, 108 batches, 1,362 pairwise constraints (total comparisons 1,383), BT-intensity vs. expert-label Spearman ρ = **0.1808** (p = 0.0083), ternary accuracy 0.3962, κ = 0.0947.

**A previous mis-attribution must be corrected (2026-09-22).** Earlier drafts attributed 0.046 to "60 of 108 batches failing to parse" and thereby claimed "protocol fix (free-string → enum-array) raised the conclusion from 0.046 to 0.1808". Batch-by-batch recomputation shows this narrative **does not hold**: 0.046 comes from the **free-string form with zero parse failures** (its 676 constraints exactly match our record), while the stored 13 parse failures **all fall in the enum-array form**; "108 batches" is the combined count of both forms' batches, not the "first-round batch count". Thus the true cause of 0.046 → 0.1808 is that **the two return forms' records were merged**—enum-array alone gives 0.1553, free-string alone gives 0.0478, and after merging 0.1808. **The free-string form's failure is not "parse failure" but that its ranking itself does not carry difficulty signal**: it gives n.s. ρ even with zero failures, which is the real reason it is not citable.

**The two citable protocols are not significantly different; we cannot claim one is better.** Placing anchored absolute rating (E1-A, ρ = 0.2195) and merged BT ranking (E1-B, ρ = 0.1808; **per the Table 5 split note this merged value must not be cited as the E1-B value, so this Δ is likewise provisional**) side by side requires giving uncertainty not just point estimates. The trace is already visible in the data: **the point estimate is Δ = 0.0385 while the bootstrap mean is 0.0699**; the absolute-rating side's mean also moves from 0.2195 to 0.2276. **The 95% interval [−0.0652, 0.2193] above may therefore be shifted as a whole**; this section **must also report a bias-corrected interval (BCa or basic bootstrap bias correction)** and **rewrite this paragraph's conclusion from that corrected interval**; until the bias correction is done, the "not significant" conclusion is **provisional only**. **Conclusion (provisional, pending bias correction): not significant.** This section can therefore only report "**both protocols give consistent significantly weak correlation**", **not** "relative judgement beats absolute rating", nor the opposite—this differs from the earlier draft's "ρ = 0.1808 still below 0.2195, so 'relative judgement beats absolute rating' unsupported": the earlier phrasing implied "the two values can be directly compared", an untested precondition, now tested and the comparison withdrawn.

### 5.5 Cold-start difficulty-estimation-driven sequencing: criterion-dependence, sign error, and λ shrinkage

This experiment was not in the original design; it surfaced when reconnecting difficulty estimation to sequencing. Its design logic: an estimator's quality should be decided not by its correlation with some label but by its ability to rank items correctly within a **pool** (same knowledge point × same ability decile, 268 pools, median size 18) by true difficulty.

**One design failure, and its value as a publishable lesson.** The first-version design aimed at absolute success rate 0.75 as target; it **degenerated**: the pool's true mean correct rate was only 0.68, unreachable at 0.75, and the oracle-calibrated target quantile q* was forced to 0.12, so all estimators degenerated to "pick the easiest item", indistinguishable from random. Switching to **quantile aiming** (target quantile shared by all estimators, oracle-calibrated) produced discrimination. We consider this itself a record-worthy adaptive-sequencing experimental-design lesson: **when the in-pool difficulty distribution differs from the global difficulty distribution, aiming at absolute success rate collapses all estimators to the same trivial solution, masking their differences**; the aiming target must be expressed as a quantile not an absolute value.

**In-pool ranking power: fusion wins only at the smallest sample size.** Under quantile aiming, the in-pool Spearman is in Table 6. At k = 10 fusion (0.5778) beats success rate alone (0.5507), +2.71 pp; but at k ≥ 25 success rate monotonically leads, and at k = 500 success rate reaches 0.9017 while fusion only 0.8321.

**Sample-size-adaptive shrinkage rule (λ's closed form).** The fusion weight must shrink with sample size: under in-pool口径 λ* drops from ~0.92 at k = 10 to ~0.20 at k = 500, fitted as

This closed form is a parametric fit to fused6's per-k empirical λ*(k); its generalizability is supported by two classes of tests—**leave-one-k cross-validation (in-interval interpolation)** (fit parameters k₀ = 27.332, p = 0.895 using only k ∈ {10, 50, 200}, then evaluate the un-fitted k = 25 and k = 100 within the fit interval [10, 200], retaining **111% / 128%** of empirical optimal gain) and **fixed-closed-form direct holdout substitution** (not fitting λ on data, evaluating on held-out half, gap from empirical-optimal-λ version ≤0.55 pp). **All numeric tables are in M1 §7.4, 7.5.** Here we record only one implication for sequencing decisions: **λ decreases monotonically with k and needs no redeployment retuning**, directly writable into the online ranker; but the applicable interval is k < 100—at k = 250 in-pool口径 the gain has dropped to +0.331 pp, and at k = 500 to +0.244 pp.

**Criterion dependence: our most important negative result (only qualitative conclusion and cross-reference to M1 §7.2 kept).** The above in-pool fusion gain on early experiments (O4/O5) was once phrased as "improvement in predictive power of difficulty estimation" (DBE 0.2207 → 0.2899 equal-weight / 0.5240 ± 0.1069 optimized weights; Junyi 0.2610 → 0.3629 / 0.4023 ± 0.0480). **This phrasing must be withdrawn**, for two layers of reasons. The first layer is **construct distance**: the expert-difficulty label vs. true-performance-difficulty Spearman ρ is only **0.4326** (Junyi ability decile 5, n = 906, ≥500 responses), i.e., the expert label explains only ~19% of the rank variance of true performance difficulty—an estimator's progress in "fitting expert labels" does not necessarily translate to progress in "predicting student performance". The second layer is **direct held-out validation**: split each item's reservoir slots into mutually exclusive estimation segment A (k rows) and criterion segment B (250 rows), so estimation error and criterion error no longer share samples; after changing the criterion to "true performance on unseen samples", **equal-weight seven-signal fusion is worse than success rate alone on all k ∈ {10,25,50,100,200} on both Junyi and DBE, fusion win rate 0.00** (Junyi −0.0267 to −0.1222; DBE −0.1752 to −0.2113; values in M1 §7.2). The resulting revised conclusion is: **the fusion gain observed in O4/O5 is a "consistency gain with expert labels", not a "predictive-power gain"**. Here we must add a qualification consistent with M1: **part of this reversal is built into the criterion choice**—the baseline "success rate alone" estimates in segment A **exactly the criterion quantity itself** (segment B's true success rate), so this design is asymptotically favourable to the baseline, consistent with the observed "larger k, larger disadvantage". Therefore we narrow the claim to "**the conclusion depends on the definition of the criterion quantity**", neither writing "fusion is useless" (its gain on the expert-label criterion is real, reproducible, and mechanistically clear) nor claiming to have identified all causes of the reversal (ability-correction criterion and exogenous-criterion two tests were truly executed by companion M1 on 2026-09-23, see M1 §7.2; §5.6 only hands off). The implication for similar work is direct: any fusion gain reported on a criterion of expert annotation or any static label must be re-tested on a criterion of held-out real performance.

## 6 Discussion

### 6.1 Ordinal action space: empirical support, exploitability, and its cost

We provide two previously missing empirical bases for "difficulty selection should be modelled as an ordinal-action problem": the expert-difficulty label's strict monotonicity at aggregate level (0.7355 / 0.6328 / 0.6160), and the asymmetry of ordered transitions (descending transition beats ascending by 12.3 pp). The former shows the "ordinal" scale is at least directionally valid; the latter shows "ordinal" is not just a scale property but carries measurable dynamic structure.

But we also give three costs. First, **scale not equally spaced**: hard and normal differ by only 1.7 pp, while on DBE the per-item口径 ρ is only 0.2207—ordinality is far stronger at aggregate than per-item level, any modelling mixing the two overestimates scale precision. Second, **structure cannot substitute for difficulty**: knowledge-tree depth vs. difficulty only ρ = +0.0785 and non-monotone, parent–child edge consistency 0.487; we explicitly refute using structure as ordinal-difficulty proxy. Third, **observed friction ≠ exploitable friction**: `descent_pair` only gets +0.0127 (~1.4σ) weak support, because transition direction is endogenous to prior outcomes. We argue these three costs jointly delimit the reasonable expectation of "ordinal-action modelling"—it is worth doing, but the gain comes from explicitly modelling the **transition structure**, not from treating difficulty as an ordinal scale.

### 6.2 Single-model difficulty prior: from "how high is correlation" down to "is this number stable, is the protocol parseable"

Our position on sub-design two differs from existing literature. The two camps debate correlation magnitude; we first ask **whether this measurement is robust, whether the protocol is parseable**, and give a negative middle answer: **only two protocols yield usable estimates** (enum-array + BT, anchored absolute rating), and the protocol-level conclusion rests on those two alone, the other two each unusable for a different reason (v1 generation truncated by `num_predict=16`; free-string **zero parse failures but rank unreliable**); and **the two usable values are not significantly different** (Δ = 0.0385, 95% CI [−0.0652, 0.2193], p = 0.34), so we claim neither "relative ranking beats absolute rating" nor the opposite. Here we record a withdrawal: earlier drafts used "same model gets four answers 0.03 / 0.05 / 0.18 / 0.22 just from different protocols" to argue "protocol robustness greater than method choice"; that argument is withdrawn in revision—two of those values come from **failed protocols**, placing them alongside usable values is **category confusion**; after revision the only claimable point is "**protocol parseability decides whether the experiment can conclude**". This finding's methodological implication holds beyond difficulty annotation—it suggests all empirical research using "LLM output structured annotation" as a data pipeline should make **return-form-stratified parse-failure rate and protocol version** mandatory report items, otherwise model-ability differences and protocol-implementation differences cannot be separated.

Under parseable protocols, our substantive conclusion is **item-type dependent, and bounded to this single model**: on DBE-like item types this model's text-perceived difficulty is of the same order as the 161,953-response behavioural evidence (0.2195 vs. 0.2207), on Chinese primary-school maths unrelated to empirical difficulty (−0.038). For engineering practice, the only actionable rule we give is negative: **do not transfer difficulty prior across item types or across models**; before enabling, estimate alignment on the **target item type and target model** at small sample. We stress this rule is independent of the tested model scale—the assessed matrix is bounded to 4 families × 3 scales (no S4_>70B tier), so we can make no claim about scale effect, nor treat this matrix result as representative of "all LLMs".

### 6.3 Consistency gain ≠ predictive-power gain

The §5.5 reversal is our most important negative result; its methodological implication deserves separate discussion. The multi-signal fusion's gain on the expert-label criterion is stable and statistically significant on both datasets (Junyi paired difference +0.1017, 95% CI [0.0726, 0.1325], one-sided p = 0.000; DBE +0.0723, 95% CI [−0.0195, 0.1649], one-sided p = 0.059—**must write that on DBE under this criterion it is only marginally significant**). Yet after switching to the held-out-real-performance criterion, the same fusion is worse than the single signal on all sample sizes.

The mechanism of this reversal is not mysterious: the signals weighted into the fusion (hint rate, time, attempt count, repeat-session rate) themselves heavily overlap with **the expert annotator's judgement cues**—experts also tend to rely on structural complexity and time cost when grading item difficulty; while "predicting the true performance of unseen students" requires characterising the ability–difficulty interaction, precisely the information the success-rate signal most directly carries. So what fusion does is partly "better mimic the annotator's heuristic".

If this explanation holds, it yields a testable prediction: **whenever the criterion itself is a human static judgement (expert annotation, teacher grading, crowdsourced label), multi-signal fusion will systematically gain; whenever the criterion is held-out-sample real performance, the gain shrinks or reverses.** We only observe the second half of this pattern on two datasets, one task; we suggest future work directly test the first half.

## 9 Conclusion

**Our headline conclusion is: consistency gain ≠ predictive-power gain.** This is the paper's most important negative result, and the core message our proposed protocol-audit method most wants to convey—any fusion gain reported on a static-label criterion should not be directly read as a difficulty-estimation predictive-power improvement. We must clarify our contribution scope: the truly generalizable contribution is **the protocol-audit method** (protocol-usability triple criterion + bootstrap retest-uncertainty quantification + LLM-annotation-experiment mandatory-reporting regime), the 4 families × 3 scales = 11 EVALUATED cells' LLM difficulty-annotation evaluation is only empirical support for this method, not an independent general "LLM difficulty-prior boundary" claim (model-related conclusions strictly bounded within the assessed annotation matrix, see §5.8 and limitation ⑬).

We rewrote the two skipped premises in adaptive learning—"can difficulty levels be treated as independent arms" and "can LLM difficulty annotation serve as prior"—into testable empirical propositions, and reported measured results on four public learning logs and one self-built simulator. **The LLM-related conclusions are bounded to this paper's measured annotation matrix—4 model families × 3 parameter scales (§5.8, 11 EVALUATED cells including F4_DeepSeek, gate exit code 0 reproducible)**, not generalising to a general "all LLMs" boundary claim; external-validity limitations (E1-B incomplete coverage, S4 tier missing, deepseek/qwen36 E1-B below comparability threshold, retest instability) in limitation ⑬; cross-model-difference reading bounded by §5.8 retest uncertainty (~±0.045). Four main conclusions:

First, **the ordinal action space has a measurable empirical basis, but the gain comes from transition structure not the scale itself**. On Junyi expert-difficulty labels' empirical success rate is strictly monotone (0.7355 / 0.6328 / 0.6160), ordered transition shows 12.3 pp climbing friction (hard→easy 0.7534 > easy→easy 0.7335 > easy→hard 0.6302); this friction's exploitability only weakly supported (descent_pair +0.0127, ~1.4σ), and knowledge-tree structure explicitly refuted as difficulty-order proxy (ρ = +0.0785, edge consistency 0.487).

Second, **a sequencing rule needing no ability prior can match ability-aware policies**. The cost of ability misjudgement is robustly positive (v1 +0.0485, v2 +0.0309, ~2.9σ), while `flow_zone` needing no ability estimation achieves 0.7119, higher than llm_correct's 0.7023 but not significantly (~1σ). Its value is bridging: M1's located **single-value mode (75% success rate, error rate 0.25)** can be operationalised as a sequencing rule needing no ability prior, flow_zone's aiming point takes 0.75. Here consistent with M1's correction—**we do not call the 80%–85% band the "optimal success-rate zone"**: M1 has declared its descriptive distribution to be a description of experienced difficulty rather than a test of any candidate optimal value (about the 85% rule it says nothing), and the 80–85% band only 19.64% and its high share bandwidth-inflated.

Third, **this single model's difficulty prior is item-type dependent, and protocol parseability matters more than the "absolute-rating vs relative-ranking" form choice**. On DBE anchored-scale absolute rating ρ = 0.2195, enum-array + BT ρ = 0.1808 (value after merging both return forms; the enum-array form alone gives 0.1553), same order as behavioural evidence 0.2207, and **the two not significantly different** (item-level paired bootstrap Δ = 0.0385, 95% CI [−0.0652, 0.2193], p = 0.34); on XES ρ = −0.038. Only two protocols yield usable estimates, and the protocol-level conclusion rests on those two alone; the other two (v1 truncated thinking text ρ = 0.0295, free-string ρ = 0.0478) not citable, where free-string's failure is **rank itself unreliable** (zero parse failures but ρ not significant), not parse failure—our early "0.03 / 0.05 / 0.18 / 0.22 four answers, protocol robustness greater than method choice" phrasing narrowed accordingly, its causal narrative (0.046 from "60 batches parse failure") withdrawn after batch-by-batch recomputation. We propose making protocol version and parse-failure rate mandatory report items for LLM annotation experiments. **Our cross-model claim bounded to §5.8's measured 4 families × 3 scales (F1_Qwen / F2_Llama / F3_Mistral / F4_DeepSeek, 11 cells), not rising to a general "LLM boundary" claim for "all LLMs"; and each cell's ρ bounded by ~±0.045 retest uncertainty (§5.8).** Our cross-model conclusion applies only to the evaluated 4 families × 3 tiers = 11 cells, must not extrapolate.
